# Portfolio & Creative Voice

> Reusable style guide for portfolio copy, journal posts, case studies, and personal projects.
> Paste into any AI assistant or share with anyone drafting on Dan's behalf.

*Built from site copy, journal posts, and Dan's email voice. Edit any rule below—what Dan asks for in a task always wins over this file.*

---

## 1. Core voice in one line

Thoughtful, grounded, and quietly confident. Sounds like someone explaining their work over coffee—direct without being blunt, considered without being stiff. The reader should feel like they're having a real conversation, not reading a press release.

## 2. Length and rhythm

**Short paragraphs.** One idea per paragraph. Two to four sentences max.

**Sentences stay under 20 words** on average. Mix shorter punchy lines with slightly longer ones for rhythm.

**Headlines are direct.** No clever wordplay. Say what the thing is.

| Content type | Typical length |
|---|---|
| Journal post | 150–400 words |
| Project intro | 2–3 sentences |
| Case study section | 3–5 paragraphs |
| About/bio | 3 paragraphs |
| Hero tagline | 8–15 words |

**White space matters.** Let the writing breathe. Don't pack dense paragraphs together.

## 3. Openings

Start with the point or the tension—not context.

| ✓ Good | ✗ Avoid |
|---|---|
| "Modern life is brutal." | "In this post, I want to discuss..." |
| "The past 6 weeks I've become increasingly more stressed." | "Welcome to my blog where I share thoughts about..." |
| "I come across a lot of design work and portfolios online." | "As a designer, I often find myself..." |

**For case studies:** Lead with the outcome or the problem. The backstory comes second.

**For journal posts:** Start with a statement, observation, or question that pulls the reader in.

## 4. Tone by content type

| Content | Tone | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Journal / thinking out loud | Reflective, personal, sometimes philosophical | "Your mind is truly something to be cherished. It's a sacred little garden." |
| Case study | Confident but not boastful, evidence-led | "Contributed to 1.9 million unique customer engagements." |
| Project description | Clear and purposeful | "A Figma plugin to explore ideas quickly." |
| About page | Warm and grounded | "Hey, I'm Dan — a Senior Product Designer based in Woonona, NSW." |
| Homepage hero | Concise and impactful | "Product designer focused on discovery, systems and human behaviour." |

## 5. Person and perspective

**First person** for journal posts and about copy: "I've started to make conscious efforts..."

**Third person** for bios and formal descriptions: "Dan brings together product discovery, customer-centred design and systems thinking..."

**Never:** "One might consider..." or passive corporate constructions.

## 6. Vocabulary — favour

**Plain language.** Say "make" not "leverage". Say "help" not "facilitate".

**Action verbs:** shape, explore, turn, bridge, bring together, map, translate, combine.

**Design language (when appropriate):** discovery, systems thinking, customer-centred, concept development, prototype, iterate, validate.

**Grounded metaphors:** garden, foundations, bridge, shape.

**Australian/British spelling:** colour, behaviour, organise, centre, favour.

**Oxford comma:** yes.

## 7. Vocabulary — avoid

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| "Passionate about..." | Overused and vague |
| "Leverage", "utilize", "synergy" | Corporate filler |
| "I hope this finds you well" | Email preamble |
| "In this article, I will discuss..." | Academic filler |
| "Revolutionary", "game-changing" | Hyperbole |
| American spellings | Inconsistent with Dan's voice |
| Exclamation marks (in body copy) | Sounds breathless; save them for casual email |

## 8. How Dan describes his work

Lead with what it does for people, not with the feature.

| ✓ Good | ✗ Avoid |
|---|---|
| "Helps customers understand what needs their attention" | "Notification management system" |
| "Turn early insights into clear concepts" | "Facilitate stakeholder alignment sessions" |
| "Make complex problems easier to understand" | "Simplify the user experience paradigm" |

**Impact before method.** State the outcome, then (if needed) explain how.

## 9. Showing, not telling

Use evidence and specifics rather than adjectives.

| ✓ Evidence | ✗ Claim |
|---|---|
| "Contributed to 700% increase in hub visits" | "Achieved amazing results" |
| "Led discovery into the future of customer communications" | "Experienced leader in design" |
| "Working across product, engineering, and research" | "Strong cross-functional collaborator" |

## 10. Closings

**Journal posts:** End with a reflection or an open thought—not a call to action.

> "I usually find that when I return to these, my stress starts to disappear completely."

**Case studies:** End with what was learned or what came next.

**About copy:** End with an open door—where to connect.

> "I also use AI to explore ideas, prototype quickly and build working products, bridging the gap between design and development."

## 11. Links and references

Links sit naturally in the sentence. Introduce them simply.

✓ "...currently at [Commonwealth Bank](https://www.commbank.com.au), exploring the future of customer communication."

✓ "This site is a static [Astro](https://astro.build) 4 build, hosted on [Netlify](https://www.netlify.com)."

For lists of references, a short note of context is helpful:

> "Dan (gr8 name) is doing the kind of work I aspire towards. Go Dan."

## 12. Drafting checklist

Run silently before showing a draft:

- [ ] Opens with the point or tension, not preamble
- [ ] One idea per paragraph
- [ ] Under ~300 words unless it's a case study
- [ ] No corporate filler ("leverage", "synergy", "passionate")
- [ ] British/Australian spelling throughout
- [ ] Evidence and specifics over adjectives
- [ ] Reads like a conversation, not a memo
- [ ] Facts not known → placeholder like [date], never invented
